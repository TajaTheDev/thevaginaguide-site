Vaginal dryness after menopause: what actually helps

By Taja Moxey, women's health educator · November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

After menopause, lower estrogen makes vaginal tissue thinner, drier and less stretchy. Doctors call it genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM), and it affects an estimated 27% to 84% of postmenopausal women. Unlike hot flashes, it usually doesn't fade on its own. The first step is a vaginal moisturizer every 2 to 3 days plus lube during sex. If that's not enough, low-dose vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA and ospemifene are effective options to ask your doctor about.

Everyone warns you about hot flashes. Almost nobody mentions the dryness, the burning, the itch, or sex that suddenly hurts. If that's you, you're far from alone, and this isn't something you just have to live with.

Why menopause causes vaginal dryness

Estrogen keeps your vaginal tissue thick, stretchy and moist. When it drops at menopause, the tissue gets thinner and makes less moisture. Your vaginal pH rises too, which means fewer protective bacteria. Your bladder and urethra are affected as well, which is why urgency and repeat UTIs often show up at the same time.

Together these changes are called genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM). You may also hear "vaginal atrophy."

Here's the part that matters most: hot flashes usually ease over the years, but GSM usually doesn't. Without treatment, it tends to stay the same or get worse. The good news is that the treatments are safe and effective, and it's never too late to start.

Step 1: Moisturizer and lube (they're different)

  • Vaginal moisturizer hydrates the tissue over time. Use it every 2 to 3 days, whether or not you're having sex. Look for fragrance-free products made for vaginal use, such as those with hyaluronic acid.
  • Lubricant reduces friction during sex. Silicone lasts longest and is great for very dry tissue. Water-based is safe with all toys and condoms. Oils can break down latex condoms.

The Menopause Society recommends these as the first step for milder symptoms. Skip anything with fragrance, "warming" or "tingling" ingredients, and avoid douches and scented washes.

Step 2: Treatments to ask your doctor about

  • Low-dose vaginal estrogen (cream, small tablet or soft ring). It treats the tissue directly, with very little reaching your bloodstream. Guidelines also recommend it to help prevent repeat UTIs after menopause.
  • Vaginal DHEA (prasterone), a nightly insert.
  • Ospemifene, a daily non-hormone pill for painful sex caused by GSM.

Had breast cancer? Start with moisturizers and lube, and talk to your oncologist before any hormone treatment.

What about vaginal laser? It's heavily marketed, but The Menopause Society says there isn't enough good research yet to know if it works or is safe long-term.

Step 3: Keep the tissue active

Regular arousal and sexual activity, with a partner or solo, helps keep blood flowing to the tissue. Pelvic floor exercises help too. A 2025 review of studies in postmenopausal women linked pelvic floor training with better arousal, orgasm and satisfaction. Start with our guide to doing Kegels correctly.

See your doctor if

  • You have any bleeding after menopause, even once. It needs to be checked
  • Sex is still painful after a few weeks of moisturizer and lube
  • You get repeat UTIs, or burning when you pee
  • You notice a sore, lump or color change on your vulva

Questions people ask

What is the best thing for vaginal dryness after menopause?

Start with a fragrance-free vaginal moisturizer every 2 to 3 days and lube during sex. If that isn't enough, ask your doctor about low-dose vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA or ospemifene.

Does vaginal dryness after menopause go away?

Usually not on its own. Unlike hot flashes, genitourinary syndrome of menopause tends to stay the same or get worse without treatment.

What's the difference between a vaginal moisturizer and a lubricant?

A moisturizer hydrates the tissue over time and is used regularly. A lubricant reduces friction during sex and is used in the moment. Most women with dryness benefit from both.

Is vaginal estrogen safe?

Low-dose vaginal estrogen treats the tissue directly with very little reaching the bloodstream, and The Menopause Society considers it effective and safe for most women. If you've had breast cancer, talk to your oncologist first.
