Leaking while lifting weights? Why it happens and how to stop it

By Taja Moxey, women's health educator · November 14, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Leaking during heavy squats, deadlifts or jumps is stress urinary incontinence: the pressure you create when you brace under a heavy load is greater than what your pelvic floor can hold back in that moment. It's very common. In studies of competitive women, roughly 4 to 5 in 10 powerlifters and weightlifters report it. Pelvic floor training, better bracing and a pelvic floor physio can reduce or stop it.

A powerlifter leaking on the competition platform went viral this month, and the comments were full of shock. Here's the thing: if you lift heavy, you probably know someone this has happened to. Maybe it's you. It's one of the most common, least talked-about parts of strength training for women.

Why heavy lifting makes you leak

When you brace for a heavy squat or deadlift, you take a big breath and tighten your core. That creates a spike of pressure inside your belly, which is exactly what keeps your spine safe under the bar. But that pressure also pushes down on your bladder.

Your pelvic floor is the hammock of muscles that keeps your urethra closed. If the pressure from above is bigger than what those muscles can resist in that split second, urine escapes. That's stress urinary incontinence: leaking caused by pressure, not by needing to pee.

It isn't a sign that you're weak. In powerlifters, leaking tends to happen at very heavy or maximal weights, and the weight that triggers it goes up as women get stronger. Strong athletes leak because the forces involved are so extreme.

How common is it?

41–49%of women powerlifters
37–54%of women weightlifters
45%of women who do CrossFit

A systematic review of studies on women in powerlifting and weightlifting found leaking in about 4 to 5 out of every 10 athletes. The deadlift was the lift most often linked to leaks, followed by the squat. High loads, high reps and competition days all made leaks more likely.

Across all sports, a meta-analysis found about 36% of female athletes leak, and athletes were far more likely to leak than inactive women. A separate review of over 4,800 women who do CrossFit found 44.5% had leaked.

And it's not just women who've had babies. Many young athletes who've never given birth leak during training too.

Why it's nothing to be embarrassed about

Leaking under a heavy bar is a pressure problem, not a hygiene problem and not a failure. It's so common in strength sports that it should be part of every women's lifting conversation. The real harm is the silence: women quit sports they love, or train dehydrated to avoid leaks, because nobody told them it's common and treatable.

What helps

  1. See a pelvic floor physio. They can check whether your muscles are weak, overly tight or just not coordinating with your breathing, and build a plan around your actual lifts. A tight pelvic floor can leak too, and Kegels can make that worse.
  2. Learn to actually contract your pelvic floor. Many women do Kegels wrong. In a large survey of women powerlifters, those who were confident they could contract their pelvic floor, or had a pelvic floor exam, had less severe leaking. Start with our guide to doing Kegels correctly.
  3. Train it consistently. Pelvic floor muscle training is the first-line treatment for stress leaks. In a Cochrane review, women with stress leaks who trained were 8 times more likely to report a cure than women who didn't.
  4. Practice "the Knack." Squeeze your pelvic floor right before the effort, the same way you would before a sneeze. Work with a coach or physio on timing it with your brace.
  5. Build load gradually. Notice which lifts, weights and rep counts trigger leaks, and progress those more slowly while your pelvic floor catches up.
  6. Don't dehydrate yourself. Cutting water to avoid leaks is common, but it hurts performance and concentrated urine can irritate your bladder.
  7. Ask about support devices. Some athletes use a vaginal support insert for competition. A pelvic floor physio or doctor can tell you if one suits you.

Leaking when you sneeze or laugh, too? It's the same mechanism. Read why you pee when you sneeze.

See a provider if

  • Leaking is getting worse or happens with everyday movement
  • You feel heaviness, a bulge or dragging in your vagina (possible prolapse)
  • You have pain, burning, blood in your urine, or a sudden urgent need to pee (could be a UTI)
  • You leak stool or gas

Questions people ask

Is it normal to pee while lifting weights?

It's very common: studies find about 4 to 5 in 10 competitive women powerlifters and weightlifters leak. Common doesn't mean you have to accept it, though. Pelvic floor training and a pelvic floor physio can help.

Why do I leak during squats and deadlifts?

Bracing under a heavy load creates a spike of pressure in your belly that pushes on your bladder. If it's stronger than your pelvic floor can resist in that moment, urine escapes.

Do Kegels stop leaking during lifting?

Pelvic floor training is the first-line treatment for stress leaks, but only if you're doing it correctly and your pelvic floor isn't overly tight. A pelvic floor physio can check which you need.

Should I stop lifting if I leak?

No. Most women can keep lifting while they work on their pelvic floor, bracing and load progression. See a provider if you notice heaviness, a bulge or pain.
