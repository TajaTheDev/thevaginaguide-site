Does cranberry juice actually help UTIs? What the research really says

By Taja, women's health educator · November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Cranberry can help prevent UTIs, but it won't cure one you already have. A 2023 Cochrane review of 50 trials found cranberry products lowered the risk of repeat UTIs in women who get them often, by about 26%. D-mannose didn't beat placebo in a large 2024 trial. If you have UTI symptoms right now, you need a test and treatment, not juice.

Every time a UTI hits, someone tells you to chug cranberry juice. Your grandma did it, your best friend swears by it, and TikTok has opinions. So does it work? The honest answer: sort of, for prevention, and not at all for treatment.

Prevention vs treatment: the key difference

Cranberry contains compounds called proanthocyanidins (PACs) that make it harder for E. coli, the bacteria behind most UTIs, to stick to your bladder wall. That can help stop an infection from starting. But once bacteria are already multiplying in your bladder, cranberry won't clear them. An untreated UTI can spread to your kidneys.

What 50 trials say about cranberry

The Cochrane review, the gold standard for evidence summaries, looked at 50 randomized trials with nearly 9,000 people. Cranberry products (juice, tablets or capsules) reduced the risk of UTIs in women with recurrent UTIs, in children, and in people having bladder procedures. In women with frequent UTIs, the risk dropped by about 26%.

It didn't seem to help everyone. There was little or no benefit for older adults in care homes, pregnant women, or people with bladder emptying problems.

Juice or capsules?

It's unclear which is better, and the ideal dose of PACs isn't set. A practical note: many cranberry "cocktail" drinks are mostly sugar water. If you go the juice route, look for unsweetened or low-sugar options, or consider a capsule that lists its PAC content.

What about D-mannose?

D-mannose had a moment, built on an early small study. Then the MERIT trial tested it properly: 598 women with recurrent UTIs, 2 g a day for 6 months, compared with a look-alike placebo. 51% on D-mannose still had a suspected UTI, compared with 55.7% on placebo, a difference within chance. The American Urological Association's 2025 guideline now says D-mannose alone may not be effective for prevention.

What does work for repeat UTIs

  • More water, if you don't drink much. Women with recurrent UTIs who drank less than 1.5 liters a day and added 1.5 liters of water had 1.7 UTIs a year instead of 3.2.
  • Methenamine hippurate, a non-antibiotic prescription your provider can offer for prevention.
  • Vaginal estrogen if you're in perimenopause or menopause. Guidelines recommend it to reduce repeat UTIs when it's safe for you.
  • Antibiotic prevention, either daily or a single dose after sex, for some people. See how to prevent UTIs after sex.

Skip the juice and call a provider now if

You have fever or chills, back or side pain, nausea or vomiting, blood in your urine, you're pregnant, or symptoms aren't improving. Those can mean a kidney infection or something that needs prompt treatment.

Questions people ask

Can cranberry juice cure a UTI?

No. Cranberry may help prevent UTIs in women who get them often, but it can't clear an active infection. UTI symptoms need testing and treatment.

How much cranberry should I take to prevent UTIs?

The ideal dose isn't established. Studies used juice, tablets and capsules. Choose low-sugar juice or a capsule that lists its PAC content, and ask your provider.

Does D-mannose work for UTIs?

In the large 2024 MERIT trial, D-mannose didn't prevent UTIs better than placebo, and the 2025 AUA guideline says D-mannose alone may not be effective.

What is the best way to prevent recurring UTIs?

Options with good evidence include drinking more water if you drink little, cranberry products, methenamine hippurate, vaginal estrogen after menopause, and antibiotic prevention for some people. Talk to a provider about which fits you.
