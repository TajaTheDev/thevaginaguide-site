Why do I pee a little when I sneeze? Stress incontinence, explained

By Taja, women's health educator · November 12, 2026 · 5 min read

Quick answer

Leaking when you sneeze, laugh, cough or jump is called stress urinary incontinence. It happens when sudden pressure from your belly is stronger than the muscles that keep your urethra closed. It's common, but you don't have to live with it: in a Cochrane review, women with stress leaks who did pelvic floor training were 8 times more likely to report a cure (56% vs 6%).

You see the sneeze coming and cross your legs like your life depends on it. Or you skip the trampoline at the kids' party. Or you laugh a little too hard at brunch and immediately regret it. Leaking is so common that we joke about it, but it's also very fixable.

What's actually happening

Your pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles that supports your bladder and helps keep your urethra (the tube you pee through) closed. When you sneeze, cough, laugh or jump, pressure in your belly spikes and pushes down on your bladder. If your pelvic floor can't hold the urethra closed against that pressure, a little urine escapes. That's stress urinary incontinence. "Stress" means physical pressure, not emotional stress.

Who gets it?

  • Anyone who's been pregnant. Pregnancy stretches and loads the pelvic floor, and that includes C-section births.
  • People in perimenopause or menopause, when lower estrogen affects tissue and muscle tone.
  • Runners, jumpers and lifters. High-impact sports load the floor. Strong athletes leak too.
  • Anyone with chronic pressure from constipation, a long-term cough, or lifting while holding their breath.

The 1-second trick: the Knack

Right before you sneeze, cough, laugh or lift, quickly squeeze your pelvic floor first, then let the sneeze out. This pre-squeeze braces the urethra right when the pressure hits. Practice it until it's automatic. Lots of women notice fewer "oops" moments within days.

The long-term fix: pelvic floor training

Pelvic floor muscle training is the first treatment guidelines recommend. UK guidelines advise at least 3 months of supervised training for stress leaks. And it works: a Cochrane review found women with stress leaks who trained were eight times more likely to report being cured than those who didn't, and they had about one fewer leak per day.

Other things that help

  • Don't cut back on water to avoid leaks. Concentrated urine can irritate your bladder.
  • Treat constipation. Straining puts constant pressure on your pelvic floor.
  • See a pelvic floor physical therapist. They can check your muscles and tailor a plan. Ask for a referral, especially after having a baby.
  • Ask about other options if training isn't enough, like vaginal support devices (pessaries) or procedures.

When to see a doctor sooner

Get checked if you feel a bulge or heaviness in your vagina, leak with a sudden strong urge to go, have pain or burning when you pee, or see blood in your urine. Those can point to prolapse, an overactive bladder or an infection.

Questions people ask

Is it normal to pee when you sneeze?

It's common, especially after pregnancy and around menopause, but it isn't something you have to accept. Pelvic floor training cures or improves stress leaks for many women.

How do I stop leaking when I sneeze?

Squeeze your pelvic floor right before you sneeze (the Knack), and do pelvic floor muscle training consistently for at least 3 months.

Can stress incontinence go away?

Yes, for many women. In a Cochrane review, 56% of women with stress leaks who did pelvic floor training reported being cured, compared with 6% who didn't.

Does leaking after having a baby go away?

It often improves over the months after birth, and pelvic floor training speeds that up. If you're still leaking a few months postpartum, ask for a pelvic floor physical therapy referral.
