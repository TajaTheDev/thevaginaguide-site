How to get your doctor to take your vaginal symptoms seriously

By Taja, women's health educator · November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Bring specifics, not just feelings: when symptoms started, how often they come back and what you've tried. Then ask directly for testing to confirm the cause, ask what else it could be, ask them to note in your chart if they decline a test, and ask for a gynecologist referral if you're not getting answers. Symptoms that keep coming back deserve a long-term plan, not another round of guessing.

"It's probably nothing." "Just try another round of this." "Some women are just prone to it." If you've heard any of those for the fifth time while your symptoms keep coming back, you are not being dramatic. You're being undertreated. Here's how to walk in and walk out with a real plan.

Why this happens so often

Appointments are short, and vaginal symptoms overlap: yeast, BV, trich, irritation and UTIs can all feel similar. That leads to guessing. And guessing often misses. In one study, only about a third of women who thought they had a yeast infection actually had yeast alone. When treatment is based on a guess, it fails, symptoms return, and it starts to feel like it's "just you." It isn't.

Step 1: show up with data

Doctors move faster with specifics. Before your visit, track for a few weeks if you can:

  • When symptoms start, relative to your period and to sex
  • What they look and smell like, and how bad they are (0 to 10)
  • What you've already tried, and whether it helped
  • How many times this has happened in the last year

Step 2: know what "recurrent" means

Using the right words gets you taken more seriously:

  • Recurrent yeast infections: three or more episodes in less than a year. These need testing and usually a longer treatment plan.
  • Recurrent BV: BV that keeps returning after treatment. Guidelines include longer suppressive options, and partner treatment is now part of the conversation.
  • Recurrent UTIs: commonly defined as 2 in 6 months or 3 in a year. The AUA guideline covers urine cultures, prevention options and non-antibiotic choices.

Step 3: use these scripts

"This is the [number] time this year. I'd like testing to confirm what it is."
"What else could this be if it isn't [their guess]?"
"If we're not testing today, can you note in my chart that I asked for this test?"
"I'd like a referral to a gynecologist (or urogynecologist)."

These aren't confrontational. They're clear. Most providers respond well to a patient who's prepared.

Step 4: ask about the long game

  • "If this comes back, what's the plan? Is there a preventive option?"
  • "Does my partner need to be tested or treated?" (For BV, a 2025 trial found partner treatment cut recurrence from 63% to 35%.)
  • "Should we do a culture to check which organism it is?"

If you still aren't heard

You're allowed to get a second opinion. Try a different provider, a gynecologist, a sexual health clinic, or a telehealth service that specializes in vaginal health. Bring your tracking notes and your list of past treatments.

Don't wait if you have

Fever, chills, back or side pain, severe pelvic pain, heavy bleeding, or symptoms during pregnancy. Those need prompt care, today.

Questions people ask

What do I do if my doctor dismisses my symptoms?

Bring specific notes, ask directly for testing to confirm the cause, ask what else it could be, request that a declined test be noted in your chart, and ask for a referral or seek a second opinion.

How many yeast infections a year is too many?

Three or more yeast infections in less than a year counts as recurrent and should be tested and treated with a longer-term plan.

What counts as recurrent UTIs?

Recurrent UTIs are commonly defined as 2 infections in 6 months or 3 in a year. That's a reason to ask about cultures and prevention options.

Can I ask my doctor to note a refused test in my chart?

Yes. Asking a provider to document that you requested a test is a reasonable, non-confrontational way to make sure your concern is recorded.
