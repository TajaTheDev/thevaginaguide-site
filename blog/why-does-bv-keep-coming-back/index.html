Why does BV keep coming back after antibiotics?

By Taja Moxey, women's health educator · November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

BV keeps coming back because antibiotics often don't fully clear the sticky biofilm that BV bacteria build on the vaginal wall, your protective Lactobacillus may not grow back fast enough, and a regular partner can pass BV bacteria back to you. In one study, 58% of women had BV again within 12 months of treatment.

You took every single pill. The fishy smell disappeared. You felt like yourself again. And then, a few weeks later, usually right after your period or sex, it's back. If that's you, hear this first: you are not dirty, and you didn't do anything wrong. Recurrent BV is incredibly common, and science finally has a good idea why it happens.

How common is it for BV to come back?

Very. In a well-known Australian study, women treated with a standard 7-day course of oral metronidazole were followed for a year. 58% had BV again within 12 months, and 69% had abnormal vaginal bacteria again. So if your BV keeps coming back, you're in the majority, not the exception.

Reason 1: the biofilm (aka the bacterial bunker)

BV isn't just "too many bad bacteria floating around." Researchers looking at vaginal tissue under the microscope found that in BV, Gardnerella vaginalis forms an adherent biofilm: a sticky layer glued to the vaginal wall with other bacteria built into it. Think of it as a bunker. Antibiotics knock out a lot of the bacteria outside the bunker, but some survive inside it and regrow once treatment ends.

Reason 2: your good bacteria never moved back in

A healthy vagina is usually dominated by Lactobacillus, the bacteria that make lactic acid and keep your pH low (about 3.8 to 4.5). Antibiotics clear BV bacteria, but they don't put Lactobacillus back. If the good guys don't repopulate quickly, BV bacteria have an open lane to take over again.

Reason 3: the partner ping-pong

This is the big one researchers kept circling. In the same 2006 study, having a regular sex partner during follow-up, or a female partner, was linked to BV coming back. Then in 2025, a major trial found that treating male partners at the same time cut BV recurrence from 63% to 35%. (We broke that study down in Should your partner be treated for BV?)

Reason 4: everyday things that tip your pH

  • Douching and scented washes. They wipe out Lactobacillus. Your vagina cleans itself; water on the outside is enough.
  • Semen. It's alkaline, so it pushes vaginal pH up after sex without a condom. That's why the smell often shows up the next day.
  • Your period. Blood has a higher pH too, which is why BV loves to reappear around your cycle.
  • Smoking. It's consistently linked with BV.

What actually helps break the cycle

  1. Finish the full course, even when you feel better on day 3.
  2. Ask about partner treatment if you have a regular partner. Bring the 2025 study with you.
  3. Use condoms during treatment and for a few weeks after, so semen doesn't raise your pH while things reset.
  4. Stop douching and skip scented products. Fragrance-free everything, outside only.
  5. Got BV 3 or more times a year? Ask about suppressive therapy. The CDC guidelines include options like metronidazole gel twice a week for 4 to 6 months after a full treatment course.
  6. Be realistic about probiotics. The most promising one so far is a vaginal L. crispatus product (Lactin-V), which lowered recurrence from 45% to 30% in a trial, but it isn't widely available yet. Over-the-counter probiotics are generally safe to try as backup, not as a replacement for treatment.

When to see a doctor

See a provider if this is your first time with these symptoms, you're pregnant, treatment didn't work at all, you have pelvic pain or fever, or it keeps coming back. Trichomoniasis and other infections can look a lot like BV, and they need different treatment.

Questions people ask

Is recurrent BV an STI?

BV isn't officially classified as an STI, but research increasingly shows it behaves like a sexually transmitted infection. Having a regular partner is linked with recurrence, and treating male partners cut recurrence nearly in half in a 2025 trial.

Can BV go away on its own?

Sometimes mild BV clears without treatment, but symptomatic BV should be treated. Untreated BV is linked with a higher risk of STIs and pregnancy complications, so see a provider if you have symptoms.

Why does BV come back after my period?

Menstrual blood has a higher pH than the vagina normally does. That temporary rise in pH can let BV bacteria regrow, especially if some survived treatment in the biofilm.

How do I stop BV from coming back?

Finish your full treatment, ask about treating your partner, use condoms while your vagina resets, avoid douching and scented products, and ask your provider about suppressive therapy if you get BV 3 or more times a year.
