Do boric acid suppositories work? What they're good for (and when to skip them)

By Taylor, women's health educator · November 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Quick answer

Sometimes. Boric acid suppositories have the best evidence for stubborn yeast infections, especially non-albicans types, where the CDC recommends 600 mg vaginally once a day for 3 weeks. For BV, they aren't a stand-alone cure, but they can be one step in a plan for BV that keeps coming back. Never swallow them, and don't use them if you're pregnant.

Boric acid has become the TikTok answer to everything down there. Fishy smell? Boric acid. Itching? Boric acid. Smell a little "off" after sex? You guessed it. I get it. When BV or yeast keeps coming back, you want something that works. And boric acid can be genuinely useful. But it's a tool for specific situations, not a daily vagina vitamin, and there are a few safety rules everyone needs to know.

What boric acid actually is

Boric acid is a weak acid with mild antifungal and antibacterial effects. For vaginal use, it comes as a 600 mg powder inside a capsule that you insert into your vagina, usually at bedtime. It's sold over the counter, but these suppositories are not FDA-approved, which means the dose and quality can vary between brands. It doesn't kill everything. Think of it as a helper that makes the vagina a harder place for stubborn yeast and BV bacteria to hang on.

Where it has real evidence: stubborn yeast infections

This is boric acid's strongest use. Some yeast infections are caused by species other than Candida albicans, like Candida glabrata, which often shrug off the usual creams and fluconazole. For those, the CDC guidelines recommend 600 mg of boric acid in a vaginal capsule once a day for 3 weeks, with cure rates of about 70%.

A review of 14 studies found cure rates between 40% and 100% with boric acid for hard-to-treat yeast. And in a 2023 study of women whose regular yeast infections had stopped responding to fluconazole, boric acid cleared the yeast in about 86%. That's why gynecologists who specialize in vaginitis keep it in their back pocket.

Where it can help: BV that keeps coming back

Here's the part TikTok usually gets wrong. Boric acid is not a proven stand-alone BV cure. Where it shows up in the research is as one step in a longer plan for recurrent BV. BV bacteria build a sticky biofilm on the vaginal wall (we explained it in Why does BV keep coming back?), and boric acid may help break that bunker apart after antibiotics have done the heavy lifting.

The CDC lists this as an option for women with multiple recurrences, based on limited data:

  1. Antibiotics first: metronidazole or tinidazole by mouth for 7 days.
  2. Then boric acid: 600 mg in the vagina once a day for 21 days.
  3. Then maintenance: metronidazole gel twice a week for 4 to 6 months.

Notice that boric acid sits between prescription steps. That's very different from "use boric acid whenever you smell something."

Before you try it: are you sure what you're treating?

BV, yeast and trichomoniasis can all cause odor, discharge and irritation, and they need different treatment. Boric acid won't treat trich, chlamydia, gonorrhea or a UTI. If you've never had a confirmed diagnosis, or this time feels different, get tested first. (Not sure which one you've got? Start with Yeast infection or BV? How to tell the difference.)

The safety rules nobody puts in the TikTok

  • Never swallow it. Boric acid is toxic if swallowed. The capsules look like vitamins, and people have taken them by mouth by accident. Store them away from your other pills, and far away from kids and pets.
  • Not if you're pregnant or trying to get pregnant.
  • Not on broken skin. Skip it if you have sores, cuts or raw, irritated skin down there.
  • No sex during treatment. It can irritate your partner's skin, and it should never end up in anyone's mouth, so oral is off the table too.
  • Expect some watery discharge. That's the capsule dissolving. Mild burning happens in fewer than 1 in 10 women. Strong burning, pain or a rash means stop.
  • Don't make it a forever habit on your own. Long-term or twice-weekly use should be a plan you make with your provider, not a guess.

When to see a doctor instead

Go to a provider if this is your first time with these symptoms, you're pregnant, you have pelvic pain, fever, sores or blisters, symptoms didn't improve after a full course, or you're getting BV or yeast 3 or more times a year. Recurrent infections deserve a real workup: a culture to identify the exact yeast species, STI testing, and a long-term plan.

And if you've been told "it's probably nothing" more than once, bring receipts. Write down every episode: dates, symptoms, what you tried and whether it worked. Questions people ask

Can boric acid cure BV on its own?

There isn't good evidence that boric acid alone cures BV. In the CDC guidelines and the studies behind them, it's used after a full course of antibiotics, as one step in a longer plan for BV that keeps coming back.

How long does it take boric acid to work?

Many women notice less odor or itching within a few days, but feeling better isn't the same as being cleared. Regimens in the guidelines run 14 to 21 days, and you should follow the plan your provider gives you.

Can I have sex while using boric acid suppositories?

Most product labels say to avoid sex during treatment. Boric acid can irritate a partner's skin, and it should never end up in anyone's mouth, so skip oral sex too.

Is boric acid safe during pregnancy?

No. Don't use boric acid if you're pregnant or trying to get pregnant. Talk to your provider about pregnancy-safe treatment instead.

Why is my discharge watery on boric acid?

Watery discharge is a common side effect as the capsule dissolves. Wearing a panty liner helps. Strong burning, pain or a rash is not normal, so stop and call your provider.
